5. Please describe the main contribution of the paper.
The authors present an adaptation of SAM3 that combines the paradigms of interactive segmentation with weakly-supervised learning using sparse scribbles as labels. The authors modify SAM3’s training objective by introducing two main contributions: (1) Partial Mask Loss that specifies valid regions where gradients can be computed using only the scribbles, and (2) Elimination of geometric constraints for the Hungarian Matcher in SAM3 where the authors adapt its loss function by discarding bounding-box dependent loss components (L_box, L_giou) and reweighting the rest of the loss components. Their contributions (1) and (2) allow training or fine-tuning SAM3 using scribbles alone, which was not possible out-of-the-box due to the sparse geometrical nature of scribbles and heavy reliance on the geometry of the original Hungarian Matcher. The authors report a competitive performance compared to text-based interactive models and previous weakly-supervised approaches using scribbles.

The paper has the following strengths:

(1) Particularly transparent and fair evaluation

The authors have put a lot of effort into making the evaluation as transparent and as fair as possible. For example, they have investigated whether foundation models (such as VoxeTell) have already seen parts of the validation data that the authors use, as these foundation models are trained on a tremendous amount of datasets, and it is difficult to compare to them without carefully considering this. This transparency is very important as it is crucial when interpreting quantitative results. Additionally, the authors also compare to both weakly-supervised and text-prompt-based foundation models to cover both paradigms and demonstrate that combining these paradigms in their approach makes sense.

(2) Well-justified design decisions

The authors justify their design decisions and provide sound explanations. One example is on page 5, where they explain that they discard the bounding box regression constraints as the bounding box derived from sparse scribbles are unrepresentative and would provide a poor learning signal in the gradients. Additionally, they propose the partial mask loss to reduce noise from the weak signals and demonstrate it with visual examples to show that it works. The justification of their decisions makes it possible to understand the contributions of the paper and to convey the main message much more convincingly to the reader.

(3) Strong motivation

The task of weakly-supervised interactive segmentation is very relevant for the medical image segmentation field as it would reduce the burden of manual labelling, which is especially important in the medical context where experts are needed for every annotation. While the marriage of weakly-supervised signals and interactive segmentations is not entirely novel [1], [2], it is still an underexplored domain, and the authors motivate it very well by applying methods to a very well-known model (SAM3).

[1] Asad, Muhammad, et al. "Adaptive multi-scale online likelihood network for AI-assisted interactive segmentation." MICCAI 2023.
[2] Roth, Holger R., et al. "Going to extremes: weakly supervised medical image segmentation." Machine Learning and Knowledge Extraction 3.2 (2021): 507-524.

Although the paper is technically sound, it has some major weaknesses:

(1) Limited scope of user interactions to text prompts

It is unclear why the authors have limited the scope of this paper to only text prompts, as SAM3 also supports scribbles, clicks, and boxes. This is particularly concerning as the authors apply their approach specifically and only to the SAM3 model. Is there a reason for this that is not present in the paper? It is also unclear how these text prompts were provided to all of the models in Table 1 - were the prompts merely the semantic categories (e.g., segmentation class to be segmented)? In that case, this would undermine a major claim of the authors that they combine the “interactive” and “weakly-supervised” paradigm. Are these models truly “interactive” if we only use one single text prompt without any refinement? The authors should explain this aspect in the rebuttal, as it is a fundamental flaw.

(2) Unclear impact of scribble supervision

How do we know how much time we save with the scribble supervision? Can the authors clarify what the impact of switching from manual annotation to sparse scribbles is (or should be)? It seems intuitive that it should be a significant reduction, but is the additional engineering effort worth the reduced annotation time? Elaborating on this question would strengthen the “weakly-supervised” contribution of this paper.


(3) Missing ablation details

How are lambda_ce, lambda_presence=20, lambda_dice=10, and lambda_focal=200 determined? It seems very disproportional (200 vs 10), so it is important to explain how these numbers were determined. Were these values derived empirically through a grid search, or is there some other reason behind them?

5. Please describe the main contribution of the paper.
This paper addresses weakly supervised interactive image segmentation using a SAM3-based framework. The main contribution is a prompt-driven approach that integrates sparse scribble annotations and text prompts to enable supervision with minimal labeling effort. The method adapts SAM3 to incorporate scribble-based spatial guidance and employs low-rank adaptation (LoRA) for efficient fine-tuning. Additionally, a partial mask loss is introduced to restrict gradient updates to annotated regions, which is intended to improve training under sparse supervision. The approach is evaluated on three multi-modal medical imaging datasets.

- Reduced annotation burden: The use of sparse scribble annotations instead of full pixel-wise masks significantly lowers annotation cost and effort, making the approach more practical for real-world medical imaging scenarios.
- Effective use of weak supervision: The introduction of a partial mask loss enables training with incomplete annotations by restricting supervision to labeled regions, allowing the model to leverage sparse scribbles without requiring dense ground truth.
7. Please list the major weaknesses of the paper. Please provide details: for instance, if you state that a formulation, way of using data, demonstration of clinical feasibility, or application is not novel, then you must provide specific references to prior work.
- Limited novelty in prompt formulation: The proposed use of scribbles can be viewed as an extension of existing point-based prompts (in SAM3), as scribbles can be reduced to sets of spatial points or sparse masks. It remains unclear whether the method effectively leverages the structural information in scribbles beyond what simpler point-based prompting would achieve. A direct comparison between scribble-based and point-based prompting is missing, making it difficult to assess the actual benefit.

- Slice-wise 2D formulation for 3D data: The method processes volumetric data using independent slice-by-slice 2D predictions, which may lead to inconsistencies across slices and degraded 3D coherence. This design choice does not fully exploit the 3D contextual information available in the data. Given that the underlying model can handle richer spatial context, it is unclear why a fully 3D formulation is not considered.
